### Section 8.2: Neural Network Optimization (in-depth)

#### How Learning Differs from Classical Optimization 

| Classical Optimization | Machine Learning Optimization |
|------------------------|-------------------------------|
| Directly minimize objective | Minimize **expected generalization error (risk)** |
| True gradient available | Noisy gradient from mini-batches |
| No surrogate loss | Use surrogate loss if true loss not differentiable |
| Stop at global minimum | **Early stopping** to prevent overfitting |

**Key Insight**: The training objective (empirical risk) is only a proxy for the true goal (generalization).

#### The "Stochastic" Advantage

Arguments for stochastic (mini-batch) optimization:
- Standard error of mean estimator: $\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$ — diminishing returns from larger batches
- Small batches provide **regularization** through noise (but require smaller learning rates)

#### Ill-Conditioning Problem

**Second-order Taylor expansion** of gradient descent update:
- Update: $-\epsilon g$ adds $\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^2 g^T H g - \epsilon g^T g$ to the cost
- This can become **positive** if $g^T H g$ is large relative to $g^T g$
- Monitor both values during training: if $g^T H g$ increases by orders of magnitude while $g^T g$ stays constant, you have ill-conditioning

#### Local Minima vs. Saddle Points

**Critical finding for deep networks**: In high-dimensional spaces, local minima become **rare** and saddle points become **exponentially more frequent** .

**Why**:
- Local minimum requires **all Hessian eigenvalues positive**
- Saddle point requires **mixed signs** (positive and negative)
- Random sign assignment → probability of all positive = $(\frac{1}{2})^d$, exponentially small in dimension $d$

**Empirical implication**: Saddle points are the more serious problem in deep learning, not local minima.

#### Cliffs and Gradient Clipping

**Cliffs**: Extremely steep regions that may appear suddenly in flat areas. Cause extremely high gradients, potentially making updates overshoot and increase loss.

**Solution**: Gradient clipping
- **Option 1**: Clip individual gradient entries at a maximum value
- **Option 2**: Clip gradient vector norm at a specific value

### Section 8.4: Numerical Stability and Initialization 

#### Why Initialization Matters

**Purpose**: Break symmetry between neurons with similar inputs—otherwise they develop identically during training.

**Heuristics** :
1. **Break symmetry**: Random initialization using high-entropy distribution (Gaussian or uniform)
2. **Bias initialization**: Usually constant values (often 0). For saturating activations, non-zero may help.
3. **Balance**: Weights large enough to keep activations numerically significant, not so large as to cause explosion

#### The "Right Balance" Problem

| Problem | Cause |
|---------|-------|
| Gradient explosion | Weights too large |
| Vanishing gradients | Weights too small (activations become 0) |

**Practical advice**: Monitor activations and gradients of all layers on mini-batches. If they vanish in specific layers, adjust initialization scale.

#### Theoretical Derivation (from Glorot & Bengio 2010)

**Assumptions** :
- Activation function symmetric with $f'(0)=1$ (linear around zero)
- Zero mean of weights and inputs
- In linear regime initially

**Forward pass variance propagation**:
$$Var[z_i] = Var[x] \prod_{i'=0}^{i-1} n_{i'} Var[W_{i'}]$$

**Backward pass variance propagation**:
$$Var\left[\frac{\partial L}{\partial s_i}\right] = Var\left[\frac{\partial L}{\partial s_d}\right] \prod_{i'=i}^{d} n_{i'+1} Var[W_{i'}]$$

**Goal**: Keep information flowing both directions → require:
- $\forall i: n_i Var[W_i] = 1$ (forward stability)
- $\forall i: n_{i+1} Var[W_i] = 1$ (backward stability)

**Compromise solution**:
$$Var[W_i] = \frac{2}{n_i + n_{i+1}}$$

**Xavier/Glorot uniform initialization**:
$$W \sim U\left[-\frac{\sqrt{6}}{\sqrt{n_j + n_{j+1}}}, \frac{\sqrt{6}}{\sqrt{n_j + n_{j+1}}}\right]$$

#### He/Kaiming Initialization (2015) 

**Why ReLU needs a different formula**: Half the activations are zero → effective variance halves.

**Derivation correction**:
$$Var[a_l] = \frac{1}{2} \cdot n \cdot Var(W) \cdot Var[a_{l-1}]$$

Setting equal to $Var[a_{l-1}]$:
$$Var(W) = \frac{2}{n}$$

**He initialization**:
- **Normal**: $W \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \frac{2}{n})$
- **Uniform**: $W \sim U\left[-\sqrt{\frac{6}{n}}, \sqrt{\frac{6}{n}}\right]$

Where $n$ = fan_in (preserves forward variance) or fan_out (preserves backward variance).

### Section 8.5: Batch Normalization 

#### The Core Insight (from Ioffe & Szegedy 2015)

**Problem**: Using gradient descent, updates for a particular layer assume preceding layers **don't change**—which is obviously false . This is called **internal covariate shift** .

**BN Solution**: Normalize activations to have zero mean and unit variance across the mini-batch .

#### Mathematical Definition

For mini-batch $B = \{x_1, ..., x_m\}$:
1. $\mu_B = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m x_i$ (batch mean)
2. $\sigma_B^2 = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m (x_i - \mu_B)^2$ (batch variance)
3. $\hat{x}_i = \frac{x_i - \mu_B}{\sqrt{\sigma_B^2 + \epsilon}}$ (normalize)
4. $y_i = \gamma \hat{x}_i + \beta$ (learnable scale/shift)

**Why the $\gamma, \beta$ parameters?** Normalization reduces expressive power. $\gamma$ and $\beta$ allow the network to learn the identity transformation if needed .

#### Key Properties

**For convolutional layers**: Normalize per feature map (not per pixel). With batch size $m$, output feature map size $p \times q$, effective batch size = $m \cdot p \cdot q$ .

**For fully connected layers**: Normalize per neuron.

#### Effects of BatchNorm 

| Effect | Mechanism |
|--------|-----------|
| **Faster training** | Reduces internal covariate shift |
| **Higher learning rates** | Gradients decrease as parameters increase (opposite of normal) |
| **Regularization** | Examples seen in context of batch, can't memorize easily |
| **Enables saturating non-linearities** | Prevents getting stuck in saturated regions of sigmoid/tanh |
| **Reduces need for Dropout** | BN provides its own regularization |

#### BN Enables Higher Learning Rates

**Standard problem**: Large learning rates scale up parameters → changes amplify through network → gradient explosion.

**With BN**: Gradients actually **decrease** as parameters increase. Therefore, higher learning rates can be used safely .

#### Experimental Results (Ioffe & Szegedy 2015 on ImageNet)

Using BN with Inception network :
- **14x fewer steps** to reach same accuracy (with 5x learning rate)
- **Better final accuracy** in 5x fewer steps (with 30x learning rate)
- Enabled training with **sigmoid** units (accuracy slightly below ReLU)

**Ensemble of 6 BN networks** outperformed previous ImageNet state-of-the-art.

### Section 8.6: Residual Connections (ResNet) 

#### The Degradation Problem (He et al. 2015)

**Finding**: Networks deeper than ~20-30 layers had **worse training error**—not just test error. This was surprising because deeper networks should be able to match shallower ones by learning identity mappings .

**Why it's surprising**: If you add more layers, in theory you can set them to learn $f(x)=x$. Deeper network should be at least as good as shallower .

**The Reality**: SGD couldn't find the identity mapping solution from random initialization.

#### The Residual Solution

**Standard layer**: $H(x)$ (learned mapping)

**Residual layer**: $H(x) = x + F(x)$ where $F(x)$ is the residual to learn

**Why this works**: If identity is optimal, simply set $F(x)=0$ (easy—drive weights to zero). If near-identity is optimal, start near optimum.

#### Key Results from the ResNet Paper 

| Result | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **Depth** | Up to 152 layers (8× deeper than VGG) |
| **Complexity** | Lower than VGG despite being deeper |
| **ImageNet error** | 3.57% (ensemble), won ILSVRC 2015 |
| **CIFAR-10** | Successfully trained 100 and 1000 layer networks |
| **COCO detection** | 28% relative improvement |

**Won 1st place in all five ILSVRC & COCO 2015 competitions** .

#### Why This Matters for Modern AI

Residual networks are the foundation of:
- **All modern LLMs** (GPT, LLaMA, Claude)
- **Transformers** (every block has residuals)
- **Diffusion models**
- **Vision Transformers**

## T2: Zhang, Lipton, Li, Smola — "Dive into Deep Learning"

### Sections 5.4, 5.5, 8.4, 8.5, 8.6, 5.6

#### Section 5.4: Initialization

**Key concept**: The "sweet spot" where activations maintain stable variance across layers.

**Monitoring recommendation**: Plot mean and standard deviation of activations per layer during training . This helps detect saturation early.

#### Section 5.5: Generalization

**Classical view**: Bias-variance tradeoff → optimal complexity point

**Modern view**: Overparameterized networks generalize despite having capacity to memorize. The optimizer's **implicit bias** selects simple solutions.

#### Section 8.5: BatchNorm Details

**BN reduces internal covariate shift**: The change in distribution of layer inputs during training .

**At inference time**: Use running averages of $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ from training, not batch statistics .

**Where to place BN**: Between linear/convolutional layer and nonlinearity (pre-activation) .

#### Section 8.6: ResNet

**The "bottleneck" design**: For very deep networks, use 1×1 convolutions to reduce dimensionality before expensive 3×3 convs.

**Plain vs Residual**:
- Plain network: Learning $\mathcal{F}(x)$ to fit $H(x)$
- Residual: Learning $\mathcal{F}(x)$ to fit $H(x)-x$

## Self-Study: Dropout (Section 5.6)

**Core Idea**: Randomly "drop" (set to zero) a fraction of neurons during each forward pass.

**Effect**:
- Prevents co-adaptation of neurons
- Forces redundant representations
- Acts as ensemble of many subnetworks

**At test time**: Multiply weights by dropout probability (or use inverted dropout).

## Key Takeaways from Reading

1. **Initialization is the first line of defense** against vanishing/exploding gradients, but it's only temporary 

2. **Batch normalization fundamentally changes optimization dynamics**—gradients decrease as parameters increase, enabling large learning rates 

3. **Residual connections provide a gradient highway** through the identity term, completely solving the vanishing gradient problem for forward/backward propagation 

4. **Optimization, not expressivity, is the bottleneck**—deep networks could represent functions but couldn't learn them without these innovations 

5. **The same principles power modern AI**—Transformers use residuals + LayerNorm (variant of BN) at every block. Without these, scaling would fail .
